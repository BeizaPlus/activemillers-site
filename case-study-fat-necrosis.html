Fat Necrosis

Open the abdomen of a patient with acute pancreatitis, and the omentum tells you what happened before you see the pancreas. Scattered across its yellow surface are small, chalky-white plaques — irregular, firm, looking like candle wax dripped onto the fat. These are the lesions of enzymatic fat necrosis, and they are the calling card of pancreatic enzyme spillage. No other form of necrosis looks quite like this. Coagulative necrosis is firm and pale. Liquefactive necrosis is soft and cavitary. Caseous necrosis is cheesy and granular. But fat necrosis is white. Chalk white. The color of soap.

That whiteness is the key to understanding the biochemistry. Pancreatic lipase, released when the inflamed pancreas leaks its enzymes into the peritoneal cavity, diffuses through the omentum and hydrolyzes triglycerides into free fatty acids and glycerol. The free fatty acids then bind calcium from the interstitial fluid, forming insoluble calcium soaps. These soaps precipitate as a solid, chalky-white deposit within the adipocyte membrane. This is saponification — literally, soap formation — and it happens in the omentum, the mesentery, the retroperitoneal fat, and anywhere else pancreatic enzymes can reach. The process is enzymatic, not ischemic: the fat cells are digested from within by lipase, not killed by oxygen deprivation. On histology, the necrotic adipocytes lose their nuclei and the lipid vacuoles become fuzzy, basophilic, and granular from the calcium deposition. The surrounding tissue mounts an acute inflammatory response, and over time, the saponified fat may be walled off by fibrous tissue or resorbed, leaving behind a scar.

Why Lipase, Not Trypsin: The Enzyme That Defines the Lesion

Acute pancreatitis releases a cocktail of digestive enzymes into the interstitium: trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase, phospholipase A2, and lipase. Each enzyme damages a different substrate. Trypsin and elastase digest structural proteins and are largely responsible for the hemorrhagic necrosis of the pancreatic parenchyma itself. But it is lipase — specifically, pancreatic triglyceride lipase — that produces the distant fat necrosis that makes the diagnosis visible on the omental surface. Lipase does not require activation by enterokinase to function (it is secreted in its active form), and it works at the lipid-water interface of adipocyte membranes. The free fatty acids it releases are the substrate for saponification. Calcium is abundant in the extracellular fluid (roughly 2.5 mM), and the affinity of free fatty acids for calcium is high. The resulting calcium soaps are insoluble at physiologic pH and precipitate in situ. This is why severe pancreatitis can cause hypocalcemia: the calcium is being consumed by saponification faster than it can be mobilized from bone. When the serum calcium falls below 7 mg/dL, tetany can develop, and hypocalcemia is one of the Ranson criteria for predicting severe pancreatitis.

Differential of White Lesions on the Omentum

Fat necrosis is not the only white lesion that can appear on peritoneal surfaces. Tuberculous peritonitis produces miliary white tubercles scattered across the omentum and peritoneum. Metastatic carcinoma can seed the omentum with firm white nodules (omental cake). Tale from surgical gloves can cause a granulomatous foreign-body reaction with white plaques. The distinction is clinical: fat necrosis appears in the setting of acute pancreatitis with elevated serum lipase (often above three times the upper limit of normal), and the plaques are soft, friable, and confined to fat-bearing surfaces. TB and carcinoma have their own clinical contexts, imaging findings, and histology. But on gross inspection alone, the chalky-white plaque on the omentum of a patient with epigastric pain radiating to the back, nausea, and a lipase of 4,500 U/L is fat necrosis until proven otherwise.

Traumatic Fat Necrosis: The Breast and the Buttock

Enzymatic fat necrosis from pancreatitis is the classic form, but traumatic fat necrosis is far more common. Any blunt injury to fatty tissue — a seatbelt across the breast in a car accident, a fall onto the buttock — can rupture adipocytes and release triglycerides into the interstitium. Without pancreatic lipase, the triglycerides are hydrolyzed more slowly by tissue lipases, but the end result is the same: free fatty acids, calcium soaps, and a firm, sometimes painful lump that can mimic carcinoma on physical exam and mammography. The classic presentation is a woman who finds a breast lump weeks after a car accident or a fall, with no memory of the injury because the trauma was minor and the lump developed slowly. Mammogram shows a spiculated mass with calcifications, and the radiologist cannot exclude malignancy. Biopsy reveals fat necrosis with foamy macrophages, chronic inflammation, and calcium deposits. The pathologist's report — "fat necrosis, no evidence of malignancy" — is one of the most welcome phrases in medicine.

Outcome

Fat necrosis is a biochemical diagnosis made visible. The chalky-white plaques on the omentum are the end product of a simple chemical reaction: triglyceride plus lipase yields free fatty acids, free fatty acids plus calcium yields soap, and soap precipitates as a solid white deposit in the place where fat used to be. The same reaction, run more slowly and with tissue lipases instead of pancreatic lipase, produces the breast lump that mimics cancer after a seatbelt injury. The calcium soaps are inert and will eventually be resorbed, but they mark the site of enzyme spillage the way a high-water mark on a wall shows where the flood reached. This illustration renders the omental surface at the moment of operative discovery: the yellow fat, the white plaques, and the invisible chemistry that turned one into the other.
